# The learning loop: interventions become policies, policies earn trust

Design ported from the parallel `relay` experiment (its strongest idea), adapted to Weaver's store. The claim is deliberately narrow: **"stored a memory" is not learning — learning is the next matching workstream's plan being different for an inspectable, attributable reason, and needing fewer human interventions without weaker verification.**

## The episode

The unit of learning is a typed episode, already captured by Weaver's existing records:

```text
proposed course (decision/pass record) → human intervention (steering, rejection, correction)
  → adopted course (superseding/new decision) → actions → evidence → evaluated outcome
```

When steering *corrects* a course — as opposed to supplying facts the coordinator asked for — the coordinator distills the correction with `propose_policy` into a **policy candidate**: a plain-language rule with typed scope (workstream tags), a constrained effect, and full provenance (source workstream, pass, steering record, and what-was-proposed-vs-corrected).

## The policy lifecycle

```text
shadow ──(cross-workstream, decision-cited, intervention-free outcome)──► active
   │  └──(negative evidence)──► contested (under review) ──┐              │
   │                                                       │              │
   └──────────── superseded (lineage kept, like decisions) ◄──────────────┘
```

- **Shadow**: appears in the projection of every workstream sharing a tag. The coordinator may apply it, and every application must be cited (`applied_policy_ids` on the applying decision) so the effect is attributable — a policy is never ambient prose with silent power. Citations are validated at decision-write time: a dangling, superseded, or scope-mismatched id is rejected, not stored (`validatePolicyCitations`, `src/policies.ts`).
- **Active**: earned, not asserted, and cross-workstream. `record_policy_outcome` names the applying decision, which must exist in the recording workstream, actually cite the policy, and not post-date the outcome. Promotion requires at least one such intervention-free outcome from a workstream **other than** the one that proposed the policy — a policy cannot certify itself on its own origin. A backfilled/seeded policy has no source workstream, so any workstream's cited, intervention-free outcome qualifies. Legacy evidence rows written before attribution existed (no `applyingDecisionId`) load and are preserved, but never qualify a promotion.
- **Contested**: negative evidence (a matching workstream still needed correction on the policy's point) sets a `contested` flag **beside** status — it never auto-demotes or auto-supersedes. A contested policy stops rendering as active guidance in the projection (it moves under a distinct "under review — do not treat as active guidance" heading) until a human resolves it, and it will not promote while contested. Resolution is explicit: **supersede** it (with a corrected replacement) or **review-clear** it (`weaver policies review-clear`, when the negative evidence was situational and the policy still sound). Positive evidence never silently un-contests.
- **Superseded**: a policy that proved wrong is replaced with lineage, exactly like decisions. Supersession is **one atomic policy-store mutation**: the replacement record and both lineage links (`old.supersededBy`, `new.supersedes`) are written in a single update, so a crash can never leave two active policies or a half-linked pair. It accepts either the text of a new replacement or the id of an existing policy to link, and rejects self-supersession, a nonexistent old/replacement, an already-superseded replacement, and any link that would form a supersession cycle. Exposed as the `supersede_policy` coordinator tool and `weaver policies supersede`.

## Doctrine: the operator's own rules outrank what the fleet inferred

A policy store holds two kinds of sentence that used to look identical. One is a rule the operator wrote down — in a rules file they maintain, in a directive they typed. The other is a coordinator's inference about how the operator wants things done. The evidence loop was built for the second and, applied to the first, gets it exactly backwards: a rule the operator states plainly has nothing to prove, and holding it in shadow until some workstream happens to cite it makes it weaker than an inference that got lucky.

That cost nine days of real work. A backfilled rule from a CLAUDE.md ("never squash-merge") sat unproven in shadow while a learned merge policy — whose statement had quietly absorbed a `--squash` flag nobody chose, picked up from wording elsewhere in the operator's config — collected fifteen intervention-free outcomes and read as the settled, evidenced answer. Every silent successful merge added evidence "proving" the whole statement, incidental flag included; the merge method appeared on no surface the operator reads; and their repeated corrections had nothing to reconcile against, because the two policies were the same kind of object.

So **doctrine** is now a class, derived on every read from provenance (`isDoctrine`, `src/policies.ts`) and never stored — a flag a writer could set would be exactly the unearned authority the class exists to keep out. Three provenances qualify: a `backfill:rules` policy (the file *is* their standing instruction), a `backfill:sessions` policy carrying the operator's verbatim quote, and a live-learned policy whose statement restates the steering directive it cites rather than building on it (`restatesDirective`, checked against the steering record's own text — a statement that adds a command, a flag, or a condition the human never said is the fleet's inference, not their rule). A teammate's `seed` is explicitly not doctrine: it lands in the importer's store to earn trust the ordinary way.

What the class changes:

- **Doctrine binds without evidence**, and only in that direction. It renders first in the projection, labelled `[doctrine/…]`, with no "unproven" apology — an untested rule is one nobody has had cause to test, not a weak one. Nothing else about the loop is suspended: evidence can still be recorded on it, and it is still refused at the same authority firewall (a rules file that says "the workstream MAY merge" is refused at import exactly as a coordinator's proposal would be — the operator writing a rule down is not the same as them granting authority for it).
- **On conflict, doctrine wins.** Where a doctrine rule and a learned policy share scope tags, the projection marks the learned one `SUBORDINATE TO DOCTRINE` and instructs the coordinator to follow the doctrine, treat the learned policy as contested, and say so (`record_policy_outcome` with `intervention_free=false` naming the doctrine, or `supersede_policy`) — never to resolve the clash by judging which reads more specific. Coordinator rule 10b carries the same instruction in the system prompt, since a coordinator that never renders the doctrine section still needs the precedence.
- **A run cannot retire the operator's rule.** Negative evidence on a doctrine policy records the contest and surfaces it, but the rule keeps binding and keeps rendering in the doctrine section — a coordinator that concludes the doctrine itself is wrong raises attention for the human whose rule it is. Only they retire it, by changing the rules file (see refresh, below) or superseding it explicitly.

## The authority firewall

Structural, not behavioral: the policy effect vocabulary is closed — `add_verification`, `narrow_authority`, `advisory`. A policy that would spend, send, merge, contact, or otherwise widen authority is *unrepresentable* in the store (`src/policies.ts`), and `widensAuthority: false` is a literal type. However confident a policy becomes, authority still comes only from the workstream's autonomy config and from egress-time revalidation. Learning can recommend; it can never permit.

Beyond the structural effect gate there is an **additional lexical gate** on the policy *statement* text (`grantsAuthority`, `src/policies.ts`), applied at **every** ingress — live `propose_policy`, backfill, seed import, and supersession's new-replacement text. Grant-shaped prose ("the workstream **MAY merge** its own PR ... only when CI is green", "reviewers **are allowed to deploy**") reads as conferring authority and is refused with a note, never converted — even when hedged with "only"/"only when", which the earlier restricting-word escape let slip. A policy may **advise how to act under an existing grant** ("only merge after CI passes" — no permission modal); it may never itself assert the grant. The gate is deliberately conservative: a statement that names a permission modal beside a grant verb is refused even if it reads advisorily, because an authority firewall must fail toward refusal. The fix is rephrasing without the modal.

## Statement and mechanism: the rule, and the way it currently gets done

`PolicyRecord.statement` is the rule in the human's terms. `PolicyRecord.mechanism` is the revisable how — the exact command, flag, threshold, or endpoint that currently carries it out. They are separate fields because the evidence loop promotes the statement, so anything folded into that sentence collects outcomes that read as proof of it. The merge policy above is the worked example: fifteen successful runs said the merge bar was sound, and not one of them was about the flag that rode along in the same sentence.

The rule for authoring, carried in the `propose_policy` tool schema and coordinator rule 10a: an execution detail you observed to work goes in `mechanism`, never in `statement`, however well it worked. The transcript-distillation prompt says the same thing for the same reason — a quote makes a candidate doctrine, so a detail smuggled into it inherits an authority the operator never gave it.

A mechanism is revisable by anyone, at any time, with no approval and no supersession (`revise_policy_mechanism`, `weaver policies mechanism <id> <text>`): changing how a permitted act is performed is not changing what was agreed, and a command that stopped working should be corrected rather than worked around. Revising one leaves the statement and the evidence untouched. Mechanism text passes a narrower gate than a statement (`assertsPermission`): a literal `gh pr merge <n> --merge` is exactly what the field is for, but "may merge" is a grant wherever it is written. Mechanisms are not exported in team seeds — a how that works on the author's machine, against their tool versions, would land in a teammate's store looking like part of the rule.

## Why this is an optimization substrate (the RL framing)

This bookkeeping is deliberately shaped like a reinforcement-learning problem so that later work can optimize over it rather than redesign it:

- **Trajectory**: the episode above — state (projection), action (proposed course), correction signal (intervention), outcome (evaluated results).
- **Action space**: policy proposal (when to distill, how to scope) and policy application (which matching policies to apply where).
- **Reward**: `humanInterventions` per successful outcome (tracked per workstream in `spend.humanInterventions`, surfaced in `weaver status`), guarded by outcome quality, execution volume, and the authority firewall — a policy that reduces interventions by weakening verification must score as a regression, not a win. A **successful outcome** is a qualified typed conclusion (`WorkstreamCore.conclusion`), never an adopted work product: `weaver stats` computes the fleet reward with conclusions as the denominator and reports adopted work products alongside as an explicit *leading indicator, not outcome success*. The scoreboard also keeps provider-capacity backoff out of the logical-failure bucket and reports pilot auto-approvals (delegated authority) separately from learned-policy effects, so a policy can never take credit for a Pilot approval.

The optimization order is fixed: the bookkeeping must be trustworthy before anything trains against it. Concretely, before any learned proposal/application model: (1) episodes must be complete (every intervention linked to the proposal it corrected), (2) attribution must be enforced (unattributed policy influence is a bug, and the actor buckets — founder, agent-session, pilot, unattributed — must stay distinct), and (3) the reward must be adversarially audited (interventions can be *good* — an approval gate firing is the system working; a provider outage is not a coordinator failure). Only then is "build agents that do RL over the policy space" — proposing better policies, scoping them tighter, pruning bad ones — a well-posed problem rather than vibes with a database.

## Backfill: seeding from pre-Weaver practice

`weaver backfill` (src/backfill.ts) seeds the store from practice that predates Weaver: deterministic parsing of CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md-style rules files, and — optionally, behind `--claude-projects` — one bounded model pass that distills durable corrections from recent Claude Code transcripts. Backfill changes where candidates come from, never what they are: every seeded policy is shadow, carries a `backfill:*` provenance variant (file § heading, or session id + quote), and earns promotion through the normal evidence loop. The authority firewall applies at import — grant-shaped text (merge/send/spend/bypass without restricting language) is refused with a note, not converted.

**Re-running refreshes rules-file doctrine rather than only adding to it.** A rules file is a living document, so a seeding pass that only ever appends leaves the store holding wording the operator abandoned beside the wording they now use — and since a `backfill:rules` policy is doctrine, the stale copy binds. On a re-run, `planRulesRefresh` (pure, so the matching rules can be argued with in tests) matches this run's parsed rules against what is stored, by section ref and then by wording: an unchanged rule is still a no-op; a reworded one **updates its policy in place**, keeping its id and `createdAt` so existing citations stay valid, journaled by the store's ordinary mutation receipt; a rule whose section no longer exists in the file is **retired** (`status: 'superseded'` with `supersededReason` and no successor — there is no replacement to point at). Identity is (section, wording) and never position: bullets have no ids, and pairing by order would renumber a whole section when one line is inserted at the top. Within a section, a candidate claims a policy on exact text, else on mutual-best word overlap above a threshold — mutual-best because without it two candidates drift toward the same policy and one silently overwrites a rule it has nothing to do with. A heavily rewritten rule therefore lands as a new candidate rather than an in-place edit, which is the conservative direction: an extra shadow policy to supersede beats overwriting a rule the operator still holds. Deletion is inferred **only** from a vanished section, never a vanished bullet — a section that is gone from the file is unambiguous, while a bullet that stopped parsing may have been reformatted or grown past the length cap, and retiring the operator's rule because our own heuristics changed their mind would be quiet damage. And a rule whose words still appear anywhere in the run is a **move**, not a deletion: renaming a heading looks exactly like deleting one, and without that case a rename would retire the rules and then the store-wide statement dedup would refuse to re-create them — the operator's rules vanishing from a store that still had their file.

A refreshed doctrine statement also **contests every non-doctrine policy sharing one of its scope tags** (`applyRulesRefresh`, one atomic mutation with the update). The blast radius is the point: those policies were inferred while the old wording was in force, and the fleet does not know which inferences the new wording invalidates. It is a contest, not a demotion — nothing is deleted and no status moves — but it does pull that guidance out of the projection until a human review-clears or supersedes it, or a coordinator reconciles it citing the doctrine. On a store with a broad tag (an org-wide scope, say), one edited rule can contest a dozen learned policies at once, so `--dry-run` reports the exact ids it *would* contest before anything is written. The transcript path has no stable ref to match on and so still only dedups.

**Narrowed limitation — a large rulebook still outruns the projection window.** A large rules file is a category the shadow pool handles badly: a project's CLAUDE.md can backfill into several hundred tag-scoped shadow policies, and `renderPoliciesForProjection` caps shadow candidates at the newest 25 (the rest render only as a "+N more not shown" line). So most backfilled rules never reach the coordinator's projection — and because promotion requires a policy to be *injected → cited → carried to an intervention-free outcome*, a rule that is never injected can never be cited and so can never leave shadow. The whole rulebook sits inert: present in the store, invisible to decisions, unpromotable. This bit in real work — a coordinator briefed a symptom-patch (a bespoke per-call flag that hid a failure instead of fixing the code that produced it) while the repo's own "never patch symptoms before finding the emitter" rule sat in the un-rendered tail. Two things keep it from recurring: the load-bearing engineering doctrine (fix the cause, not the symptom; prefer the system's existing general mechanism) is inlined directly into the coordinator prompt as rule 17 rather than left to a policy that might not render, and every worker still receives the target repo's full CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md verbatim as conventions. The doctrine class removes the unpromotable trap: a rules-file policy binds without ever being cited, renders in its own section ahead of learned policies, and outranks them on overlap — so a rule the operator wrote down is no longer weaker than an inference that got lucky. What survives is the window: doctrine is capped at the newest 25 in the projection, with the remainder counted in a "+N more" line and fully listed by `weaver policies`. So the tail of a several-hundred-rule CLAUDE.md still does not reach a given pass, and the durable fix is unchanged — a repo's static rulebook is *conventions*, injected wholesale when a workstream is repo-scoped, rather than exploded into hundreds of individually-capped candidates. Tracked in "What's deliberately not here yet".

## What's deliberately not here yet

- Automatic intervention classification (correction vs. fact-supply vs. approval) — the coordinator judges this today; a classifier is future work and its errors must be inspectable.
- Confidence scores — evidence counts are stored raw; scorecard math belongs with a proper decision-lineage scorecard design, not improvised here.
- Cross-tag generalization — a policy applies where its tags say, never further; broadening scope is a human or explicitly-superseding act.
- Repo-convention injection into the coordinator — a target repo's static rulebook (CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md) reaches workers as conventions but reaches the coordinator only through the policy store, where the doctrine class now makes each rule bind but the projection still shows only the newest 25 (see Backfill above). Repo conventions should be injected into the coordinator's projection wholesale when a workstream is repo-scoped, keeping the policy store for genuinely *learned* corrections rather than a mirror of a rulebook.
- Narrowing the doctrine-refresh contest — a refreshed rule contests every non-doctrine policy sharing a scope tag, which is right when the fleet cannot tell which inferences the new wording invalidates and blunt on a store where one tag covers everything. A narrower rule (contest what the two statements actually talk about) needs a way to compare meaning that is not a word-overlap heuristic, so today it is the operator's call, with `--dry-run` showing the radius first.

## Team seeds

`weaver policies export --author <name>` produces a sanitized seed file (statements, scope tags, effect, short origin label — no ids, no evidence, no intervention summaries since session-derived ones can quote private transcripts, no absolute paths, and superseded policies stay home). `weaver policies import <file>` lands every seeded policy in shadow with `source: 'seed'` provenance naming the author; the importer's own evidence loop governs promotion, their supersessions outrank the seed with lineage, dedup makes re-import a no-op, and `grantsAuthority` refuses authority-shaped statements at the door. Sharing distributes guardrails and vocabulary — never trust, and never authority.
